Oil fields out at sea a vast in size, sometimes more than 100 miles long, albeit they are extremely thin in comparison. They are generally made up of the 3 layers as follows:
Layer 1 - a gas layer on top.
Layer 2 - the oil layer in the middle.
Layer 3 - the water layer at bottom.
In order for an oil company to work effinciently and effectively they require vital statistics on a regular basis. Once an oil field has been dicovered work is quickly underway to ascertain, not only how much oil that field pocesses but to additionaly guage how much water and gas is also contained within it. If an oil feild is considered to hold a sufficient amount of oil and a commencement of that extraction is proceeded, then the oil companies re uire to be kept up to date with all the nessessary variables concerning that field. They need to know what pressures are building up through the water,gas and oil in the field. As the oil is being extracted over time they also need to know how much reserves of oil are left in the feild. There is also the concern that in order to get the most out of each of these feilds, precise evaluations of where to drill is of the utmost importance. A lot of money can be wasted by drilling in an inadequate position in the feild, or by spending too much time and money getting to the oil at a certain location which proves to be less rewarding in the amount of extracted barrels of oil than perhaps a far more obtainable one would have been. One of the methods in which oil companies can view this information is in the form of a 3D model. The model is produced as a series of blocks of volume of oil saturated rock. Another problem is that these blocks are irregular in shape and can also contain faults. Facts and figures are produced by sizmic radar of the sea bed and when a potential oil feild is found facts and figures can be entered into the simulator. If the facts and figures are kept up to date then the simulator will show resonably precise visual representation of what is actually encapsulated in that oil feild.

The problem arises as how to best display a 3D model of an oil feild at a considerably fine-scale when firstly computers have generally been lacking in processing power and the fact that some of these oil feilds are extremely large. If when viewing a 3D model of an oil reservior, even if one were to zoom all the way into a single pixel on the screen, this would still prove to be far too course, as that single pixel would still represent a vast volume of the oil feild, perhaps still several miles accross. Some of the models being simulated at present contain anything up to 50 million of these blocks.With advancements in technology however, the introduction of multi-core computers are beginning to become common place within businesses and homes. This is resulting in volumes of data which one such computer can process greatly increase which has lead to 3D simulators being able to store larger models. In the foreseeable future models of up to 100 times larger than those previously modeled could be represented in a 3D simulation. The problem still remains however. How does one zoom into these blocks to such an extent whereby they can be effectively veiwed and evaluated so as and to such to allow oil companies to more precisely pin-point where to drill, find faults in the rock formation and to generate a more exact account of the reserves of oil contained in each of the blocks.

The school of Computing Science at Glasgow University has extensive experience in working within the feild of computer graphics and 3D models. A technique developed by the department uses a system not totally dissimilar to the Laplacian Pyramid system. This will form the basis for showing vast 3D models at a far more detailed view while the development of an effective coding algorithm will allow for quick uploading times of the model producing the best optimum manoeuvrability on the screen of the model without any delay or buffering times being endured.
